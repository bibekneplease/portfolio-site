ITIL in Real Life: Working the Service Desk Queue

How incident priority, impact, urgency, SLAs, communication and escalation fit together during a busy enterprise support day.

BY BIBEK CHAUDHARY　·　7 MIN READ　·　PRACTICAL GUIDE

Perspective

These are practical service-delivery observations, not a substitute for your organisation's incident policy, priority matrix or contractual SLA definitions.

1. Start with impact, not whoever follows up most often

A queue becomes difficult when every request feels urgent. Establish who is affected, which business service is unavailable, whether there is a workaround and what the operational or customer impact is. A single-user issue and a site-wide outage should not be treated as equivalent simply because both arrive as tickets.

2. Separate priority from noise

Use the organisation's impact-and-urgency matrix. VIP status, repeated calls or strong language may affect communication expectations, but should not replace the documented priority rules. If the business impact changes, reassess priority and record why.

3. Own the next action

  • Restate the issue in plain language and confirm the affected user, device, location and service.
  • Run safe, repeatable checks within your access and role.
  • Record findings as you go: timestamps, error messages, tests and outcomes.
  • If another team is needed, provide a concise handover with a clear question or requested action.
  • Keep the user informed at the agreed cadence, even when the technical investigation has not changed.

4. Protect the SLA by managing the work transparently

Know what the SLA measures—response, restoration, resolution or another contractual milestone. Do not assume a ticket is paused simply because it is assigned elsewhere. Follow the local rules for pending states, vendor dependencies and customer updates. Escalate early when the remaining time is at risk.

5. Write notes that another technician can use

A useful ticket note answers: what happened, who or what is affected, what has been checked, what changed, what is the current state, and what happens next. Avoid vague entries such as “checked network” or “user advised”. State the actual check and result.

6. Recognise recurring incidents

When the same symptom appears repeatedly, link related incidents where the tool and process allow it. Look for a common device model, site, recent change, application version or dependency. A workaround may restore service, but recurring patterns deserve problem-management attention and root-cause investigation.

7. A practical ticket update format

Impact: users / sites / service affected

Symptoms: exact behaviour and start time

Checks: action performed and observed result

Current status: restored / workaround / investigating

Next action: owner, dependency and expected update

Closing thought

Good service desk work is not measured only by ticket volume. It is the combination of sound triage, clear communication, accurate records, controlled changes and reliable follow-through. That is what makes support predictable for users and useful to the wider IT team.
